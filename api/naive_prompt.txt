######################################################################

Wall Street analysts DO NOT analyze slide decks in a vacuum. Institutional
investors actively cross-reference company disclosures against live Medical
Affairs intelligence, regulatory actions, and external competitor moves:

1. COMPETITOR LABELS & CLINICAL DIFFERENTIATION: When a competitor drug is
   approved or reads out, analysts scrutinize the exact label constraints
   (e.g., fasting/food requirements for oral PCSK9 inhibitors vs. Leqvio's
   biannual physician-administered profile; disability progression misses
   by competitor BTK inhibitors like fenebrutinib vs. remibrutinib).
2. CLINICAL TRIAL REGISTRY TRACKING: Analysts monitor ClinicalTrials.gov for
   sample-size upsizing, primary completion date shifts (e.g., Abelacimab
   slipping from 2027 to 2028), and endpoint hierarchy alterations.
3. PATENT LITIGATION, EXCLUSIVITIES & SETTLEMENTS: Analysts track District Court
   decisions (e.g., Delaware court rulings on generic radioligand therapies
   like Lutathera), Hatch-Waxman settlements, and exact Loss of Exclusivity (LOE)
   dates post-pediatric exclusivity (e.g., Kisqali Q3 2031 vs 2032).
4. ACCELERATED APPROVAL PRECEDENTS: Analysts challenge management on whether
   Phase II biomarker data (e.g., Votoplam in Huntington's, Del-brax in FSHD)
   can support early accelerated filing vs. requiring full Phase III studies.

Cross-reference all disclosures against the provided External Medical Affairs
Intelligence section and live market data.

######################################################################
SPECIFICITY CALIBRATION (CRITICAL — READ BEFORE GENERATING QUESTIONS)
######################################################################

Real institutional analysts do NOT ask macro/thematic questions. They ask
drug-by-drug, endpoint-by-endpoint, number-by-number questions that could
only be asked by someone who has already built a financial model off the
disclosed data. Every question you generate must meet this bar.

MANDATORY SPECIFICITY RULES:

1. THE "NIGHTMARE SCENARIO" RULE: When you identify a topic (e.g., an upcoming trial readout), do not ask "What are your expectations?" You must formulate the most technically aggressive version of the question. (e.g., "What happens to the Horizon trial data if 15% of patients drop-in to an off-label GLP-1, and how is the trial statistically powered to handle that?").

2. DEEP CLINICAL MECHANICS: NEVER ask about "pipeline timelines" in general. Name the compound, the indication, and ask about exact statistical powering thresholds, biomarker correlations (e.g., correlating KHDC1L to DUX4 for accelerated filings), or specific Mechanism of Action (MoA) nuances (e.g., brain penetration, annualized relapse rate benchmarks).

3. MODEL-BREAKING FINANCIALS: NEVER ask "how are you containing costs?" Ask for exact DCF inputs. (e.g., "Is the current gross margin of X% the exact trough we should build our models from going forward?"). Look for accounting anomalies (e.g., Did Q2 growth include a 1% inventory stocking anomaly due to a new SAP system, and did that fully reverse in Q3?).

4. EXACT REGULATORY/IP DATES: NEVER ask "How will you handle generics?" Ask about precise legal battles or exact dates. (e.g., "Can you confirm the exact month and year Kisqali loses pediatric exclusivity?" or "How will you defend Lutathera against the recent generic clearances in Delaware court?").

5. COMPETITOR/LABEL INTEL: When asking about competition, reference specific competitor trial data or label quirks, not general "competitive pressures." (e.g., "Given the competitor's newly approved oral PCSK9 inhibitor requires strict fasting, how will your sales force exploit that specific label requirement?").

6. NEVER name a category — always name the asset.
   Wrong: "How does management plan to address generic competition for key products?"
   Right: "With US generics expected for Entresto, is the [X%] gross margin the right base to build from in H2?"

7. HISTORICAL PATTERN MATCHING: Use the historical patterns shown in the tables to match the level of clinical and financial specificity expected from these analysts. Use actual drug names, trial names, and figures from the provided text.
